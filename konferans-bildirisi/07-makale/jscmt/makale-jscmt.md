%%ABSTRACT
This study aimed to document a reproducible production protocol for a fully bio-based composite in which waste eggshell powder, the major phase, is bound by agar and glycerol, and to characterise its mechanical, small-flame and water-absorption behaviour as a non-load-bearing interior surface material. Eggshell powder (below 150 µm) was added to an agar–glycerol solution cooked at 80 °C, cast in silicone moulds and dried for eleven days at about 23 °C. Five formulations were tested, three in compression (50 mm cubes) and two in three-point bending (180 × 60 × 20 mm, 120 mm span), with three specimens per formulation; small-flame and 24 h water-absorption observations were made on single specimens. Mean compressive strength rose from 2.55 to 4.10 MPa in proportion to the solids-to-water ratio of the mixture (0.37 to 0.59) at a constant mineral share of 66.7% of the dry mass. Halving the glycerol content turned a material too compliant to test into one with a nominal flexural strength of 2.33 MPa and a flexural modulus of 192 MPa. Specimens self-extinguished about 4 s after removal of a small flame, and a shellac-coated specimen gained 0.7% in mass in 24 h of immersion. Compared with sources that report strength and modulus together, the composite exceeds heat-pressed mycelium composites in strength, stiffness and elastic strain energy at maximum stress (14.1 kJ/m^3^), falls below the minimum requirements for particleboard and medium-density fibreboard (MDF) on all three, and exceeds them in elastic strain capacity (1.2%). In both mechanical tests it deformed extensively without fragmenting. Strength alone is therefore an inadequate criterion for this class of materials, and strength, modulus and failure mode should be reported together. The dataset consists of means of three specimens without dispersion, on nominal dimensions, and supports a controlled follow-up series but not specification.

# 1. Introduction

Hen eggshell is generated continuously by food processing, catering and households, and most of it is discarded. With the shell making up about 11% of the mass of an egg, the quantity arising worldwide has been estimated at 7.8 million tonnes a year {@skorczewska2022}; in Europe up to 150 000 tonnes arise annually, and landfilling is usually the only outlet {@vilarinho2022}. What is discarded is a comparatively pure mineral, about 94% calcite with about 1% each of magnesium carbonate and calcium phosphate and about 4% organic matter {@ngayakamo2022,skorczewska2022}, and with a specific gravity of 2.07–2.50 {@ngayakamo2022}. These properties have made eggshell a widely examined secondary raw material for sustainable construction materials.

Eggshell powder has been used to replace part of the cement in concrete {@teara2019,teara2020} (reviewed in {@nandhini2022,ngayakamo2022}), as a lime substitute in soil stabilisation {@diana2021}, as a mineral filler in hot-mix asphalt {@masued2019}, as a replacement for limestone in ceramic wall tiles {@vilarinho2022} and as an addition to fired clay bricks {@ngayakamo2020}. In polymers it has been compounded into poly(vinyl chloride) {@skorczewska2022}, polyurethane foam {@victor2019}, polylactic acid {@cree2023} and a partially bio-derived epoxy {@owuamanam2021} (reviewed in {@owuamanam2020}); further uses range from sintered ceramic–metal composites {@bilici2023,yonetken2022} to calcium phosphate for bone scaffolds {@shafiei2019}. In every case reported, eggshell replaces part of an existing mineral ingredient or is added to a polymer at a loading of a few to some tens of per cent: it is the minor phase, and the continuous phase is Portland cement, lime, bitumen, a fired or sintered body or a synthetic polymer. Where the matrix is itself bio-based, as in a corn-starch film {@vonnie2022}, the product is a filled film. A cast solid in which eggshell is the major phase and the binder is a native polysaccharide gel has, to the author's knowledge, not been reported.

Agar is a polysaccharide extracted from red algae that dissolves in hot water and sets on cooling to a thermoreversible gel {@armisen1997}, so that a mineral powder dispersed in the hot solution can be held in place without a hardener or a curing reaction. Agar has been studied almost entirely as thin packaging films, which are brittle and moisture-permeable unless they are plasticised, blended or reinforced {@mostafavi2020,rhim2011}. In the most systematic study, agar–glycerol–water films of 32 formulations ranged from 0.11 to 39.11 MPa in tensile strength and from 3.9% to 115.8% in elongation at break, with higher glycerol contents raising ductility at the expense of strength and stiffness {@hernandez2022}. Agar has not, to the author's knowledge, been characterised as the binder of a thick, highly mineral-filled cast solid loaded in compression and bending, which is the loading relevant to an interior surface.

Two further difficulties concern designer-made bio-based materials. The first is procedural: material-driven design starts from the properties of a material {@karana2015}, and designers who produce their own materials {@rognoli2015,ayala2017} depend on being able to repeat them, yet production parameters are often reported incompletely, as has also been noted for mycelium composites {@jones2020,girometta2019,elsacker2019}. The second concerns the criterion of evaluation. Studies of eggshell-filled polymers report tensile and flexural strength, modulus and impact energy {@owuamanam2020}; the filler typically raises stiffness {@skorczewska2022,cree2023,owuamanam2021}, and strength may fail to exceed that of the unfilled matrix {@owuamanam2021}. Judged by strength, a soft bio-based composite always appears as a weak version of whatever it is compared with, yet strength records only the stress at which a material ceases to carry load, not how much deformation it accommodates before that point or what happens after it. Two established concepts address these questions: the modulus of resilience of materials science, the elastic strain energy per unit volume, *U*~r~ = *σ*~y~^2^/2*E* {@callister2018}, for which the ratio *σ*~f~^2^/*E* is the index used to rank materials by elastic energy storage {@ashby2011}; and resilience in systems ecology, the capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and persist {@holling1973}, which Holling later distinguished from engineering resilience, the speed of return to equilibrium {@holling1996}.

This article derives from the author's doctoral dissertation {@unal2025}, whose subject is spatial resilience; its materials component is reported here. The aim is to document a reproducible production protocol for a fully bio-based eggshell–agar composite, to characterise it with reference to published test standards, and to derive an evaluation criterion suited to this class of material. Five research questions are addressed: which formulation variable governs compressive strength (RQ1); how plasticiser content affects flexural behaviour (RQ2); how the composite compares with conventional interior boards (RQ3) and with other bio-based composites (RQ4); and whether strength is an adequate evaluation criterion for such materials (RQ5). The working hypothesis is that the composite can be produced repeatably under ambient conditions and that its performance as an interior surface is governed by deformation capacity rather than by compressive strength. The material is considered throughout as a non-load-bearing interior finish for sustainable building construction; no structural use is proposed.

# 2. Materials and Methods

## 2.1 Materials

Eggshells were collected over more than a year from a single patisserie (Filika Pasta Evi, Çayyolu, Ankara, Türkiye), which also showed that one local source can sustain production at this scale. The binder constituents were agar powder [[grade; supplier, city, country]] and glycerol [[purity; supplier, city, country]], mixed with water [[tap or distilled]], which is the processing medium and leaves the material on drying. Shellac [[type; supplier, city, country]] was used as a surface finish for the water-absorption specimen only. Gelatine, starch and vinegar were tried as auxiliary constituents during development and are not part of the formulations reported.

## 2.2 Production protocol

Shells were freed of adhering membrane and organic residue, heated in a laboratory oven [[model; manufacturer, city, country]] at 120 °C for 20 min (decontamination and drying; sterility was not verified), cooled, dried to constant mass, ground in a laboratory mechanical grinder [[type; manufacturer, city, country]] until the powder appeared uniformly fine, and passed through a 150 µm sieve [[manufacturer, city, country]]; only the passing fraction was used (Fig. 1a, b). The particle-size distribution below the sieve limit was not measured. Water and glycerol were mixed to a uniform liquid, and agar powder was added a little at a time under continuous manual stirring, since agar added in bulk forms lumps that do not redisperse. The mixture, in an open vessel, was heated to 95 °C and held until the agar had dissolved completely; the temperature was then lowered to 80 °C and cooking continued with stirring while the viscosity rose (Fig. 1c), for a total heating time of 55 min. Dissolution was completed before the viscosity was allowed to build, because a mixture that thickens around undissolved agar sets to an uneven gel. Towards the end of the cook, with the binder at 80 °C, the sieved powder was added in increments under continuous stirring (added earlier, the mineral settles), and heating was stopped when the mixture was uniform, viscous and still pourable.

The mixture was poured into silicone moulds [[supplier, city, country]] and levelled; silicone allows the partly set piece to be released without a parting agent. The mould geometry followed the test: 50 mm cubes for compression and flat moulds for the flexure, flame and absorption specimens. The pieces remained in the moulds for four days, were demoulded and were kept for seven further days at about 23 °C, out of direct sunlight and draughts (Fig. 1d); no oven or other forced drying was used after the cook, so eleven days passed from casting to a finished specimen. The pieces shrink appreciably as the water leaves; shrinkage was not measured. Stirring was manual, and shear rate and mixing energy were not controlled. The water lost by evaporation during the cook, the pouring temperature, the relative humidity and the interval between shellac coats were not recorded.

## 2.3 Formulations

Five formulations were prepared, three for compression (C1–C3) and two for flexure (F1, F2) (Table 1). The solids-to-water ratio, *S*/*W*, is the mass of the non-volatile constituents (eggshell, agar and glycerol) divided by the mass of water in the batch as weighed out, since evaporation during the cook was not measured. The dry-basis shares are the masses of the three non-volatile constituents as percentages of their sum and describe the composition of the dried solid. The formulations were arrived at empirically and no factorial design was intended, but their recalculated proportions have a clear structure. In the compression series the batch mass is constant at 445 g and the mass of eggshell is exactly twice the combined mass of agar and glycerol in all three formulations, that is 66.7% of the dry mass, while the water content falls from 73.0% to 62.9% of the batch (*S*/*W* from 0.37 to 0.59). In the flexure series the masses of eggshell and agar are identical and glycerol is halved, from 60 g to 30 g, with water raised by 30 g to keep the batch at 770 g. The binder solutions are concentrated: agar amounts to 9.6–13.4% of the liquid phase, beyond the range of 0.5–8% covered by the film-forming mixtures in an earlier study, where higher agar contents gave a thick, rubber-like material unsuited to tensile testing {@hernandez2022}.

## 2.4 Test methods

The mechanical tests were carried out at the Advanced Construction Materials Research Laboratory of Hacettepe University (Ankara, Türkiye) on [[testing machines: manufacturer, model, load-cell capacity, city, country]]. Three specimens were tested per formulation in compression and in flexure; the values reported are means of the three, individual values were not retained, and standard deviations cannot be given. The flame, absorption and mass determinations are single observations. Each test refers to a published standard for its principle, but none was carried out in full accordance with it; the departures are listed in Table 2.

*Compression.* Cubes cast in 50 mm moulds were loaded between flat platens to maximum load. Because the cubes shrink on drying, the loaded cross-section of each dried cube was measured at the time of testing and entered into the machine, which reported the stress at maximum load; those dimensions were not logged. The test follows no named standard, and strain was not instrumented.

*Flexure.* Three-point bending used the configuration and the stress formula of ASTM D790-17 {@astm_d790}. Specimens were cast in moulds of 180 × 60 × 20 mm and tested on a span of 120 mm. The flexural strength was calculated as

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{f}} = \frac{3FL}{2bd^{2}}$$

where *F* is the maximum load, *L* the span, *b* the width and *d* the depth, the last two taken as the mould dimensions (*b* = 60 mm, *d* = 20 mm). Deflection was recorded by the machine, and the flexural modulus *E*~f~ was reported by the machine from the initial linear part of the load–deflection record. Three features depart from the standard. The span-to-depth ratio was 6:1 where the standard specifies 16:1, so that shear contributes to the measured deflection. The specimens carry regularly spaced transverse kerfs on the face placed in tension, whereas Eq. (1) assumes a solid rectangular section. And the dimensions in Eq. (1) are those of the mould, not of the dried specimen. The values of *σ*~f~ and *E*~f~ are therefore nominal: they characterise the kerfed panel as a component and are not constants of the bulk material.

*Small-flame exposure.* The response to a small flame was observed under exposure conditions taken from the single-flame source test of ISO 11925-2 {@iso_11925}: a flame applied to the edge of the specimen at 45° for 15 s. Dried specimens of 40 × 50 mm were held in metal tongs and exposed to the flame of a hand-held gas lighter, once at an upper and once at a lower corner, and the time to ignition, the time to self-extinction after removal of the flame, flame spread, melting and dripping were noted. The standard requires specimens of 250 × 90 mm, a specified burner and a test enclosure; the observation is therefore indicative and supports no classification.

*Water absorption and mass.* Water absorption was determined by the 24 h immersion procedure of ASTM D570-98(2018) {@astm_d570} on a single specimen of 40 × 50 mm with an initial mass of 10 g, finished with three coats of shellac. The specimen geometry is not that of the standard and no uncoated specimen was tested, so the result describes the coated surface and not the composite. The mass of one C3 cube was also recorded.

## 2.5 Derived quantities, statistics and benchmark data

For the flexural result, two quantities were derived on the idealisation of linear-elastic behaviour up to maximum load. The first is the elastic strain capacity, given by Eq. (2),

$$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}}{E_{\mathrm{f}}}$$

the outer-fibre strain that corresponds to the flexural strength at the measured modulus. The second is the modulus of resilience evaluated at the flexural strength, given by Eq. (3),

$$U = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}{2E_{\mathrm{f}}}$$

which is the elastic strain energy per unit volume at the outer fibre at midspan, and half of the index *σ*~f~^2^/*E* of Ashby {@ashby2011}. Where a yield point is defined, Eq. (3) with the yield stress is the modulus of resilience of the textbooks {@callister2018}; here the flexural strength takes its place, and *U* is used as an index for comparison, not as a measured property, and is referred to as the resilience index. For a rectangular beam in three-point bending, the elastic energy density averaged over the span is *U*/9, a geometric factor common to all materials.

The relation between mean compressive strength and *S*/*W* was examined by ordinary least-squares regression on the three formulation means. The slope was tested against zero with a two-sided *t*-test at a significance level of 0.05, with one residual degree of freedom (*n* − 2), and the fit is reported as a description of the trend and not as a model. The dry-basis shares of agar and glycerol were treated in the same way.

Benchmark data were restricted to sources whose content could be checked directly. For flexure, the derived quantities were computed only where strength and modulus are given for the same material: the nine mycelium composites tested in three-point bending by Appels et al. {@appels2019}; the minimum requirements for particleboard of type P2 in EN 312 {@en_312} and for general-purpose medium-density fibreboard (MDF) in EN 622-5 {@en_622}, in the thickness ranges of over 13 to 20 mm and over 12 to 19 mm respectively; and, as a stiff and brittle mineral reference, concrete of strength class C20/25, for which EN 1992-1-1 {@en_1992} gives a mean tensile strength of 2.2 MPa, a flexural tensile strength of (1.6 − *h*/1000) times that value for a section of depth *h* in millimetres, and a modulus of 30 GPa. Measured flexural strengths of 12.5 mm gypsum plasterboard were taken from Ranachowski et al. {@ranachowski2020}, who report no modulus. For compression, values were taken from Elsacker et al. {@elsacker2019} for mycelium composites and from Ngayakamo et al. {@ngayakamo2020} for fired clay bricks containing eggshell. The benchmark values come from different specimen sizes and test arrangements and, in the case of the standards, are minimum requirements and not measurements; they position the composite and do not constitute a controlled comparison. All derived values were computed with a script that is available from the author.

# 3. Results and Discussion

## 3.1 Compressive behaviour and the effect of water content

Mean maximum load rose from 3.89 kN for C1 to 6.80 kN for C3, and mean compressive strength from 2.55 to 4.10 MPa, an increase of 61% (Table 3). Dividing the mean load by the mean stress gives the loaded area implied by the output of the machine: 1525, 1378 and 1659 mm^2^, or 55–66% of the 2500 mm^2^ face of the mould. For a square section this corresponds to an edge of 37–41 mm and to a linear drying shrinkage of roughly 19–26%. These figures are back-calculated from rounded means and are not measurements, and they do not vary systematically with water content, but they confirm that the stresses refer to sections much smaller than the mould. The ranking of the formulations does not depend on them: referred to the face of the mould, the loads give 1.56, 1.72 and 2.72 MPa.

Strength increased with the solids-to-water ratio of the mixture (Fig. 2). The least-squares line through the three means, Eq. (4), is

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{c}} = 6.93\,(S/W) + 0.04$$

with *σ*~c~ in MPa, *R*^2^ = 0.994 and *p* = 0.047 (one residual degree of freedom). The intercept is negligible, so that strength is in effect proportional to *S*/*W*: the quotient *σ*~c~/(*S*/*W*) lies between 6.9 and 7.2 MPa for all three formulations, and a line through the origin with a slope of 7.0 MPa reproduces each mean to within 2.2%. A straight line in the water mass fraction of the batch fits equally well (*R*^2^ = 0.999), whereas the dry-basis shares of agar and glycerol show no comparable relation (*R*^2^ = 0.72 in both cases, *p* = 0.36).

Within the range examined, compressive strength therefore followed the water content of the mixture and not its mineral content (RQ1). This reading does not rest on the regression. With three points that lie almost on a line, a fit would be as good against any quantity that changes steadily from C1 to C3, the batch mass of eggshell included: in this series the mass of eggshell is an exact linear function of the mass of water, and the two cannot be separated statistically. The reading rests on the composition of the dried solid. Water leaves the material, and what remains in C1, C2 and C3 is two parts of eggshell to one part of binder in every case; the cubes differ in the amount of water that had to leave and, to a small degree, in the division of the binder between agar and glycerol. That division cannot account for the trend. C3 has the highest glycerol-to-agar ratio of the three, 0.22 against 0.14 and 0.13, and the lowest share of agar, which on the evidence of the flexural series below and of agar films {@hernandez2022} should soften the material; yet C3 is the strongest.

The likely mechanism is densification. A mixture with less water has less volume to lose on drying and can be expected to consolidate to a solid with less porosity and fewer shrinkage defects; in agar films, too, stiffness rises as the water content falls relative to agar {@hernandez2022}. The proportionality between strength and *S*/*W*, with an intercept indistinguishable from zero, is at least consistent with the limiting case that a mixture without solids has no strength. But the porosity, density and shrinkage of the dried cubes were not measured, the water content as cast is unknown, and three formulation means without dispersion cannot establish a law. The answer to RQ1 is therefore a well-supported hypothesis: at a fixed ratio of mineral to binder, compressive strength is governed by the water content of the mixture. Its practical corollary is that the route to higher strength in this system is less water, not more shell, up to the limit, not explored here, at which the mixture can no longer be poured.

No instrumented stress–strain record is available. As observed during the tests, the maximum load was not followed by an abrupt drop; the cubes shortened and barrelled while remaining in one piece (Fig. 3). The deformation at maximum load was estimated by eye at about 10% of the height. This figure is an observation and is not used in any calculation.

## 3.2 Flexural behaviour and the effect of plasticiser

The two flexural formulations behaved in different ways. F1, with 60 g of glycerol (18.8% of the dry mass), deflected under the loading nose without developing a measurable resistance; the specimens could be bent by hand, and no strength value was obtained. F2, with 30 g of glycerol (10.3%), carried a mean maximum load of 310 N, which Eq. (1) turns into a nominal flexural strength of 2.33 MPa (*L* = 120 mm, *b* = 60 mm, *d* = 20 mm); the machine reported a flexural modulus of 192 MPa. At large deflection the kerfs open on the tension face and the specimen folds without separating into two parts, and a kerfed panel can be bent by hand into an arch (Fig. 4; a displacement transducer bearing on the lower face of the specimen is visible in Fig. 4a). For F2, the elastic strain capacity (Eq. 2) is 1.21%, equivalent on the linear idealisation to a midspan deflection of 1.45 mm on the 120 mm span, and the resilience index (Eq. 3) is 14.1 kJ/m^3^ at the outer fibre, 1.6 kJ/m^3^ averaged over the span, which amounts to about 0.23 J of elastic energy in the specimen at maximum load. All of these inherit the nominal character of *σ*~f~ and *E*~f~.

The flexural series isolates the plasticiser more cleanly than its design suggests (RQ2). Between F1 and F2 the masses of eggshell and agar are equal and only the glycerol differs, with 30 g more water in the F2 mixture. If the trend of the compression series holds, more water would weaken F2, not strengthen it, so that the confounding variable acts against the effect observed. Halving the glycerol content, from 0.86 to 0.43 parts per part of agar, was thus enough to turn a material that could not be tested into one with measurable strength and stiffness, in the direction found for agar films, in which glycerol raises ductility and lowers strength and modulus {@hernandez2022}. The magnitude of the effect cannot be stated: one of the two levels produced no number, and the transition lies somewhere between them. The compression formulations, at 0.13–0.22 parts of glycerol per part of agar, lie well on the stiff side of it.

Taken together, the two series point to two levers: the water content of the mixture, which sets strength within the stiff range, and the glycerol content, which sets whether the material is stiff or pliable at an unchanged mass of eggshell and agar. That is of direct use to a designer who produces the material by hand. The data indicate the two levers but do not isolate them from each other, and mapping the glycerol transition would require a series in which glycerol is varied in small steps at a constant water content (Section 3.6).

## 3.3 Small-flame response, water absorption and recorded mass

Under the flame the specimen ignited after about 10 s. When the flame was removed, burning ceased within 4 s. Charring was confined to the area of flame contact (Fig. 5), and the flame did not spread across the surface in either exposure. Smoke and a characteristic smell were noted; the material did not melt or drip, and it kept its shape outside the charred zone.

The shellac-coated specimen gained about 0.7% in mass in 24 h of immersion, that is about 0.07 g on 10 g, with slight swelling. It remained intact, but at points where the coating was thin or incomplete the surface softened and material was lost locally, so that the coating, not the composite itself, governs behaviour in humid conditions; shellac has been used in the same role on agar films {@phanthe2008}. The figure refers to a coated specimen and to a non-standard geometry and should not be compared with values for uncoated materials.

A mass of 173 g was recorded for one C3 cube. Referred to the 125 cm^3^ volume of the mould, this would correspond to 1380 kg/m^3^, but the figure is not used because it cannot be reconciled with the recipe: the non-volatile constituents of the whole C3 batch in Table 1 weigh 165 g, so that a dried cube cast from that batch cannot weigh more. Either the cube was weighed before it had dried, or the recipe masses refer to a different batch size than the one from which the cube was cast. The mass is therefore reported as an unresolved record, and no density is claimed for the dried material.

## 3.4 Position among interior boards and bio-based composites

Table 4 and Fig. 6 set the flexural result against benchmark data, selected as described in Section 2.5. The comparison with conventional interior boards (RQ3) is unfavourable on every count but one. The nominal flexural strength of F2 is 21% of the minimum required of P2 particleboard and 12% of that required of general-purpose MDF; its modulus is 12% and 9% of the respective requirements. Since boards in service exceed their minimum requirements, the real gap is wider. The composite cannot replace a wood-based board wherever the board spans, carries shelves or holds fixings. Standard 12.5 mm gypsum plasterboard, the usual interior lining, has a measured flexural strength of 2.6 MPa across and 4.7 MPa along the direction of production {@ranachowski2020}; the composite, at 2.3 MPa, lies just below the weaker direction. The one respect in which it exceeds the wood-based boards is elastic strain capacity, 1.2% against 0.7% and 0.9%.

Against other bio-based composites the position is different (RQ4). In compression, the 2.6–4.1 MPa of the C series compares with 0.35–0.45 MPa reported for mycelium composites on flax tow {@elsacker2019}, an advantage of roughly six- to twelve-fold, and is of the same order as the 3.12 MPa of clay bricks containing eggshell and granite waste fired at 900 °C {@ngayakamo2020}. In flexure, F2 has 2.7 times the strength of the strongest and 2.4 times the modulus of the stiffest of the nine mycelium composites of Appels et al. {@appels2019}, including those consolidated by heat pressing, and its resilience index is between 1.7 and 11 times theirs.

The two families are not substitutes for each other. The composite is a mineral-rich solid, two-thirds of its dry mass being calcite, whose specific gravity is 2.07–2.50 {@ngayakamo2022}; its density was not reliably determined (Section 3.3), but it can be expected to lie well above that of mycelium composites, which is 60–390 kg/m^3^ {@appels2019,elsacker2019}, and these are valued above all as insulation {@jones2020}. At the other end of the range, polylactic acid filled with eggshell powder reaches a flexural strength of 67 MPa and a flexural modulus of 3.4 GPa {@cree2023}, some 29 and 18 times the values of F2, but it is a thermoplastic with a minor mineral filler, a different class of material and of process. Nor is the water absorbed by mycelium composites in 24 h, 24–30% {@elsacker2019}, comparable with the 0.7% measured here, which refers to a coated specimen and to a different test. What distinguishes the present material within the bio-based family is therefore not a single property. It is the combination of a process that needs a heat source, a mould and time with mechanical values that lie between those of grown composites and those of filled thermoplastics. The composite is thus weaker and softer than the boards it might sit beside, and stronger and stiffer than the grown bio-based composites with which it shares a family, and that is the position from which the question of evaluation arises.

## 3.5 Strength, resilience and failure mode

The benchmark data allow the question of the evaluation criterion (RQ5) to be put concretely, by ranking the same materials on three quantities (Table 4). By flexural strength, the composite stands above the mycelium composites, below thin-section concrete and plasterboard, and far below the wood-based boards. By elastic strain capacity the order changes: concrete, at 0.012%, falls two orders of magnitude below everything else; the wood-based boards follow at 0.7–0.9%; the composite, at 1.2%, stands with the heat-pressed mycelium composites (1.1–1.8%) and below the non-pressed ones (3.2–7.3%). By the resilience index the order changes again: concrete 0.2 kJ/m^3^, mycelium composites 1.3–8.1, the composite 14.1, particleboard 37.8 and MDF 90.9. A material that is weaker than concrete in bending stores some seventy times more elastic energy per unit volume before its strength is reached, because its modulus, which counts against it in any comparison of stiffness, stands in the denominator of Eq. (3).

Two conclusions follow, and they should be kept apart. The first is that strength alone misdescribes the material. On strength it is a poor relation of concrete and plasterboard; on strain capacity and stored energy it belongs to a different family, that of compliant materials which accommodate deformation. This is information a designer needs, and it is available only when strength and modulus are reported together for the same specimen, which, as the search for benchmark data showed, is the exception in the literature on bio-based materials. The second conclusion is that the index does not make the composite competitive with wood-based boards, which exceed it in strength, stiffness and stored energy alike. The index changes what kind of material this is understood to be. It does not turn a weak material into a strong one.

The index has, moreover, a limit of its own, which the two meanings of resilience make clear. The modulus of resilience describes recoverable deformation, energy absorbed and returned without damage. It is the material counterpart of what Holling calls engineering resilience, stability near an equilibrium steady state {@holling1996}. The most distinctive observation of this study lies elsewhere. In compression the cubes barrelled and stayed whole; in bending the panels folded and did not part. This is behaviour beyond the maximum load, and it corresponds to the other sense of the word: the capacity to absorb a disturbance and persist {@holling1973}. A panel that deforms and stays in one piece can be taken down and replaced; one that shatters cannot, and sheds fragments as it fails. Eq. (3) is blind to this difference. It assigns the same value to a material that fractures at the end of its elastic range and to one that goes on deforming.

An evaluation suited to this class of material therefore needs three entries, not one: the strength, which bounds the load; an elastic index such as *ε*~el~ or *U*, which requires the modulus and describes recoverable deformation; and the mode of failure, which describes what remains once the strength is exceeded. The present data supply the first, supply the second only as a nominal index, and supply the third as an observation. Quantifying the third, by the energy absorbed beyond maximum load, the residual strength or the recovery on unloading, is the experiment that would turn the proposition into a criterion. With that qualification, the second clause of the hypothesis is supported: what characterises this composite as an interior surface material is its capacity to deform, and compressive strength is the least informative of the quantities measured. The first clause is supported in a limited sense. The protocol yielded testable specimens of all five formulations, but repeatability was not quantified and reproduction by another laboratory has not been attempted.

## 3.6 Application envelope, limitations and further research

The conditions under which the material could be used follow from the results. The strengths measured are sufficient for self-weight and handling and for nothing more, which confines the material to non-load-bearing surfaces. Its behaviour in water depends on the integrity of the coating, which confines it to dry interiors. Sensitivity to water is, however, shared by the incumbent lining material: a cycle of immersion in water and drying at room temperature reduced the flexural strength of plasterboards by 70–80% {@ranachowski2020}, and plasterboard is nonetheless used throughout dry interiors. Such sensitivity defines a condition of use and not a disqualification. The material ignites under a small flame within seconds, even though it then extinguishes itself, and it has no reaction-to-fire classification under EN 13501-1 {@en_13501}, without which it cannot be specified as a wall or ceiling lining. Hull et al. {@hull2011} attribute the fire-retardant action of mineral fillers to heat absorption through endothermic decomposition, to the increased heat capacity of the residue and to the increased heat capacity of the gas phase through the water or carbon dioxide released. Since the calcium carbonate of eggshell decomposes only between about 650 and 770 °C {@skorczewska2022}, the first and third of these would arise late in the present material, if at all; the likelier contribution of a mineral share of two-thirds is a smaller proportion of combustible matter and an inert residue. None of this was measured.

The large drying shrinkage argues for small cast units rather than large sheets, and the behaviour at failure argues for demountable units that can be exchanged singly on reversible fixings. The dissertation develops this into a system of slotted modules assembled without adhesive {@unal2025}; the proposal has not been built at full scale or tested, and it is mentioned only as the direction in which the measurements point. The environmental case is likewise a potential and not a result. The mineral phase is a waste diverted from landfill, the binder and plasticiser can be obtained from biological sources, and no temperature above 120 °C is needed, and none above 95 °C once the shell has been prepared. Against this stand a drying period of eleven days, the energy of a 55 min cook, and the open question of how an agar-bound material behaves in the presence of moulds and at the end of its life. No life-cycle assessment was carried out.

The evidence behind these conditions is limited in ways that Table 5 sets out, together with the experiment that would remove each limitation, and these experiments define the directions for further research. Three limitations qualify every number in this article. Dispersion is unknown, because only means were retained. The dimensions of the dried specimens were not logged, so that the compressive stresses cannot be recomputed and the flexural values are nominal. And the formulations were not designed to separate the variables, so that the effects of water and of plasticiser are indicated and not isolated. The first four rows of Table 5 could all be addressed by one new series, made with the same protocol and tested on the same equipment, and the loading–unloading and post-peak records of row 7 would quantify the failure mode that Section 3.5 identifies as the most distinctive behaviour.

# 4. Conclusion

A production protocol for a fully bio-based cast composite of waste eggshell powder, 59–67% of the dry mass, in an agar–glycerol binder has been documented, and the material has been characterised as a non-load-bearing interior surface. At a constant mineral share, compressive strength rose from 2.55 to 4.10 MPa in proportion to the solids-to-water ratio of the mixture, and halving the glycerol content turned a material too compliant to test into one with a nominal flexural strength of 2.33 MPa and a modulus of 192 MPa. The composite is weaker and softer than particleboard and MDF but exceeds mycelium composites in strength, stiffness and elastic strain energy (14.1 kJ/m^3^), and its most distinctive behaviour, deforming extensively without fragmenting, is captured by no strength value. It is therefore recommended that strength, modulus and failure mode be reported together for such materials, and hypothesised that the energy absorbed beyond maximum load ranks bio-based surface materials by their service behaviour better than strength does. That hypothesis, the effects of water and of glycerol, and the properties left unmeasured here (dispersion, dried dimensions, reaction-to-fire classification and durability) are the subject of a controlled follow-up series; until then the results justify no specification.
